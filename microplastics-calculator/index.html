    3. Check the Primary Science Behind These Rules

    Boiling hard water traps plastics—but plastic kettles add billions back

    Yu, Zeng et al. (2024, Environ. Sci. Technol. Lett.) showed boiling hard water (>120 mg/L CaCO₃) encapsulates 80–90% of micro/nanoplastics inside limescale crust—if you strain the scale out afterward (soft water removed only ~25%). However, Shi et al. (2025, npj Emerging Contaminants) demonstrated that polypropylene electric kettles shed millions to billions of new nanoplastics per mL during boiling (worst in the first 10–50 boils, and never reaching zero).

    Why standard loose-carbon pitchers are NOT recommended for microplastics

    Cherian et al. (2023, Polymers, Univ. of Toronto) tested common pour-through pitchers. Pitchers using only loose granular activated carbon (GAC) and ion-exchange resin lacked a tight physical barrier and sometimes had more plastic particles in filtered water than unfiltered input. By contrast, pitchers with a 0.2 µm physical membrane removed 94–100% of PET fragments and 78–86% of PVC fragments.

    What NSF/ANSI 401 certifies—and the nanoplastics certification gap

    NSF/ANSI 401 is the primary independent certification for microplastic reduction, testing particles between 0.5 µm and 1 µm (requiring ≥85% reduction, often 95–99%+). Currently, there is no official consumer certification for nanoplastics (<0.1 µm). Reverse osmosis (~0.0001 µm pores) blocks them by physics, though poorly maintained downstream plastic tanks/tubing can re-introduce trace particles.

    Bottled water & glass bottles: what studies (and critiques) show

    Qian et al. (2024, PNAS) estimated ~240,000 plastic particles/L in bottled water (~90% nanoplastics), though follow-up PNAS correspondence noted analytical debates over lab procedural blanks. Separately, France's ANSES (2025) found glass-bottled drinks averaged ~100 microplastics/L—not from glass itself, but from polyester paint flaking off metal caps during sealing. Filtered tap water in a reusable glass or stainless bottle avoids both.

    Why boiling is unsafe if lead, nitrates, or PFAS are present

    Both the U.S. EPA and CDC warn that boiling kills germs but does not destroy heavy metals or chemical pollutants. As clean water turns to steam, non-volatile contaminants like lead, nitrates, and PFAS stay behind and become more concentrated. Use certified chemical filtration (NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 RO) instead.

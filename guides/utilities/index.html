5. Four Core Findings & Honest Methodological Caveats

1. Household Power Bills Jumped +$69.2B/yr in Five Years

U.S. residential electricity spending climbed from $192.66B (2020) to $261.82B (2025) as national average rates jumped +31.0% (13.15¢ → 17.23¢/kWh)—lifting the typical U.S. monthly electric bill from $117.46 to $150.68 even as average usage dipped slightly.

2. For-Profit IOUs Raised Rates Nearly Twice as Fast as Public Power

Weighted residential rates rose +33.8% at Investor-Owned Utilities (13.25¢ → 17.78¢/kWh) versus +18.8% at Municipal utilities (14.09¢), +19.4% at Public Utility Districts (13.44¢), and +19.3% at Rural Cooperatives (14.09¢).

3. Municipal Utilities Deliver 3.5× Fewer Blackout Minutes

In 2025 EIA reliability filings covering 96.7% of traditional residential customers, Municipal utilities reported a median 57.3 min/yr of outages (37.5 min routine non-storm), versus 205.7 min/yr (109.0 min routine) at Investor-Owned Utilities. Rural Co-ops averaged 232.4 min largely due to long, sparsely populated rural distribution lines.

4. Homes Pay More Than Double Industrial Electricity Rates

Nationally, residential households paid 17.23¢/kWh in 2025 versus 8.51¢/kWh for industrial customers (2.02×). While high-voltage industrial delivery legitimately costs less per kWh than residential neighborhood lines, families bear the brunt of fixed distribution, wildfire, and grid-upgrade riders.

Important Nuances & Honest Caveats (Read Before Citing):
  • Legacy Public Hydro & Tax Differences: Some low-cost municipal utilities—especially in upstate New York (NYPA Niagara hydro) and the Pacific Northwest (Bonneville Power Administration)—benefit from legacy federal/state hydroelectric allocations and tax-exempt municipal bond financing. Meanwhile, Investor-Owned Utilities pay property/income taxes and shareholder returns (typically 9–11% ROE) approved by state PUCs.
  • Wildfire, Urban Undergrounding & Line Density: California IOUs (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E) maintain vast wildfire-prone rural transmission lines across mountainous terrain, whereas city utilities like SMUD or Seattle City Light serve denser urban footprints. Similarly, Rural Cooperatives report higher SAIDI minutes primarily because they average far fewer customers per mile of wire.
  • Bundled vs. Delivery Accounting Safeguard: In deregulated/CCA markets (CA, IL, MA, NY, OH, PA, CT), distribution utilities report Part A (Bundled) for default-supply customers and Part C (Delivery) for wires-only customers. We never mix Delivery-only prices into full retail rates—we compute every rate strictly from full-service Bundled sales while summing Bundled + Delivery households for accurate utility footprint size.
  • Exclusions Documented: We exclude Behind the Meter third-party solar leasing firms (e.g., Sunrun, Tesla) and EIA's small-utility Short Form 861S state adjustment rows (which represent just 1.9% of U.S. households and do not break out residential vs. commercial revenue).
